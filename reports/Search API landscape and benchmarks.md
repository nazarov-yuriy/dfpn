# Harnesses, not indexes, decide search-API rankings

**Bottom line.** As of 5 November 2026 the web-search-API market for AI agents is scored on two kinds of test that rank providers differently: retrieval-only measurements (does one call return the answer or a relevant list) and agent-in-the-loop measurements (does a fixed model, given search and fetch tools, finish a hard task). Only two independent boards hold the harness fixed (Artificial Analysis holds everything but the provider fixed; Vals varies model and tool); a third, OpenBenchmarks, is self-described independent with undisclosed operators. On the Artificial Analysis Search Index **Exa auto and Perplexity medium tie at 80** with Octen at 77, Parallel and Brave at 75 (data 19 Oct 2026, GPT-5.6 Luna agent, 1,700 tasks), and on the Vals Web Search Index **GPT-5.6 Sol with Keenable leads at 49.08%** but only 0.63 points ahead of Claude Fable 5 with Exa at less than half the cost (21 Oct 2026). Keenable scores well because it owns a 100B-document index served at roughly 150–340 ms, prices at $1–4 per 1K, and ships a token classifier ("Dynamic Snippets") that halved result tokens and, in its own replication of the AA harness, lifted BrowseComp from 56.6% to 67.3%; what it has not disclosed is how its first-stage retrieval or ranker works. Exa scores well because a link-prediction-trained neural retriever over its own crawl, a custom 256-dimension binary-quantized vector database, a 2026 move to hybrid dense-plus-sparse fusion, and query-relevant "highlights" let agents finish with 7.6 searches per task against 9.5–15.4 for rivals; its known weaknesses are keyword-exact lookups, the highest per-task cost in competitor harnesses, and latency well above its advertised figures. A single Common Crawl snapshot (2.16B pages, October 2026, monthly, no full-text index, no ranking signals, JavaScript not executed, 26% of the top 1,000 sites carrying robots.txt rules for CCBot) is one to two orders of magnitude smaller than the commercial indexes' claims and lacks every layer that makes a search API usable at query time. Every vendor's own table ranks itself first; the published numbers that matter are dated snapshots under named harnesses, and the same product's BrowseComp score spans 33.7% to 91.8% (Exa) or 32% to 75% (Parallel Turbo) depending on who ran it. For a Tavily-reranking proxy such as dfpn, the external evidence says the retrieval-only gains it reports are real and in line with what reranking can deliver, that SimpleQA is a saturated Wikipedia-coverage test, and that agent-loop gains are capped by Tavily's coverage, by per-call latency, and by the fact that retrieval accounts for only a minority of agent errors.

Conventions used throughout: **[vendor]** = published by a company selling one of the compared products or grading its own; **[independent]** = a third-party evaluator with no product in the comparison; **[academic]** = benchmark authors; **[journalist]**; **[practitioner]** = developers in forums or blogs. Where the wave-2 verification pass returned CORRECTED or UNVERIFIABLE, the corrected value is used or the figure is marked unverified. Prices are USD per 1,000 requests unless stated.

## Four benchmark families, and only two independent boards

The benchmarks used on search APIs fall into four families, and almost all of them were designed for models, not retrieval backends. Short-answer factual recall is tested by SimpleQA, SimpleQA Verified, AA-Omniscience and FreshQA; multi-hop "needle" browsing by BrowseComp and its -Plus and -ZH variants, FRAMES, WebWalkerQA, SealQA, GAIA and HLE-with-tools; exhaustive synthesis by DeepSearchQA, WideSearch, Mind2Web 2 and LiveResearchBench; and classical ranking by BEIR/MTEB and MS MARCO Web Search ([SimpleQA paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04368v1), [BrowseComp](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.12516v1), [DeepSearchQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20975v1)). The field has converged on three of them as marketing lingua franca: SimpleQA or its Verified subset for single-hop, BrowseComp for multi-hop, and DeepSearchQA because Artificial Analysis adopted it. The 2026 newcomers (LiveBrowseComp, LiveNewsBench, LiveSearchBench, Keenable's NEEDLE) are explicit reactions to the saturation and contamination problems of the static sets.

What each benchmark actually measures is more specific than its name suggests. SimpleQA's 4,326 questions were built so that at least one of four GPT-4-class completions was wrong, must be time-invariant, and must be backed by two source domains; OpenAI's own count is that **Wikipedia is a source for about 3.5k of the 4.3k questions** (roughly 81%), followed by fandom.com (410), ac.uk (154) and imdb.com (121) [academic, Nov 2024] ([SimpleQA paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04368v1)). The grader is a prompted classifier with an estimated 3% inherent dataset error. Google's SimpleQA Verified paper traces the narrow source distribution to "substantial human rater biases", notes that 119 questions (2.7%) ask for founding dates of Colombian municipalities, and states plainly that **"Enabling tools on SimpleQA Verified results in near perfect performance"**, so the benchmark is only meaningful without tools [academic, Sep 2025] ([SimpleQA Verified](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07968v1)). BrowseComp's 1,266 questions were kept only if GPT-4o with browsing, o1 and an early Deep Research all failed and five Google searches did not surface the answer; humans with two hours solved 29.2% and gave up on 70.8% [academic, Apr 2025] ([BrowseComp paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.12516v1)). DeepSearchQA's 900 prompts ask for single values or exhaustive sets along a causal chain, graded as F1 over answer items by Gemini 2.5 Flash; the paper reports a ~15-point "last mile" gap between F1 and fully-correct rates [academic, Jan 2026] ([DeepSearchQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20975v1)). BrowseComp-Plus replaces the live web with a fixed 100,195-document corpus precisely because commercial APIs are "dynamic and opaque"; with the agent fixed, moving GPT-5 from BM25 to Qwen3-Embedding-8B raised accuracy from **55.90% to 70.12%** and cut search calls by one to three per query [academic, Aug 2025] ([BrowseComp-Plus](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06600v1)).

The agent-loop versus retrieval-only distinction matters because the two rank the same providers differently. OpenBenchmarks states that "the top provider changes with tool setup, for example Exa deep with fetch versus Perplexity with snippets only" [independent, Oct 2026] ([OpenBenchmarks](https://openbenchmarks.com/web-search)). Brave was first of eight on AIMultiple's top-5 relevance test yet last of eleven at the AA launch, and rose from 65 to 75 only in the separate "LLM context" configuration AA added ([AIMultiple](https://aimultiple.com/agentic-search); [AA launch article](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/search-api); [AA leaderboard](https://artificialanalysis.ai/agents/search-api)). Perplexity was seventh of eight on AIMultiple with 11-plus-second latency and tied first on AA. Tavily and Firecrawl rank far lower on NEEDLE (43% and 50%) than on AA or OpenBenchmarks because NEEDLE never fetches pages, so their extract-oriented payloads earn nothing there ([NEEDLE](https://keenableai.github.io/needle/)). Agent-loop scores are a joint property of the model, prompt, tool set, turn budget and provider; the choice of which tools the agent gets changes winners, and Parallel itself notes that Brave's 75 on AA was earned without the `web_fetch` tool that Parallel, Exa and Tavily received ([Parallel Brave comparison](https://parallel.ai/articles/brave-search-api-vs-parallel.md)).

The agent can also mask the search. LiveBrowseComp found that more than half of agent queries originate from the model's own hypotheses and that retrieved evidence is used less than a third of the time; blocking the evidence documents dropped average pass@4 from 26.1 to 6.2, which means static-benchmark agent scores partly reflect parametric knowledge [academic, May 2026] ([LiveBrowseComp](https://huggingface.co/papers/2605.28721)). DeepWeb-Bench attributes only 12–14% of frontier-model errors to retrieval [academic, May 2026] ([DeepWeb-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21482)). Exa's ATLAS post makes the same point from the vendor side: hiding the top seven of every ten results retains 81–89% of an agent's score on BrowseComp, WideSearch and DSQA, and at least one of GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol or Claude Opus 5 could recall 48–61% of those benchmarks' answers without search, versus 9% for ATLAS [vendor, Oct 2026] ([Exa ATLAS](https://exa.ai/blog/atlas-benchmark)). Benchmarks that still discriminate between backends share two traits: they require recall over many items (DSQA, WideSearch, OpenBenchmarks multi-hop) or post-cutoff, noisy evidence (SealQA, LiveBrowseComp, NEEDLE, company-news lookups). These are the places where index coverage and freshness, rather than Wikipedia retrieval, do the work.

## Independent boards agree on little beyond "search helps"

Two independent boards and one self-described independent board hold the harness fixed and vary the provider, and they do not agree on a winner. Artificial Analysis launched its Search Index on 18 August 2026 with "Same Agent, Different Search": a fixed GPT-5.6 Luna (medium) agent in AA's Stirrup harness, with `web_search` (native provider payload, up to 10 results), `web_fetch` (text-only, 15-second timeout) and `finish`, a 25-turn budget, contamination filtering of benchmark-hosted URLs and canary strings, and the same GPT-5.6 Luna as grader [independent] ([AA methodology](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/search-api)). At launch the table read **Parallel advanced 75, Exa auto 74, Firecrawl 73, Parallel basic 73, Exa fast 68, You.com 68, Parallel turbo 67, Keenable pro 67, Keenable realtime 67, Tavily basic 66, Brave 65**, with the no-search baseline at 33; Keenable realtime had the fastest search call (0.34 s) and lowest time per task (15.1 s), while Tavily basic had the highest search cost ($126 per 1K tasks) [independent, 18 Aug 2026, verified] ([AA launch article](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/search-api)).

Vals' Web Search Index measures something different. It varies both the model and the search tool, includes each model's native search, removes domain tools such as SEC EDGAR and CourtListener, and scores the final answer of expert finance (450 FAB v2 questions, three-judge jury) and legal (208 tasks, GPT-5.4 judge against attorney rubrics) tasks [independent, 21 Oct 2026, verified] ([Vals board](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/web_search)). The board reads GPT-5.6 Sol + Keenable 49.08% at $8.84 per task; Claude Fable 5 + Exa 48.45% at $3.48; Claude Fable 5 native 46.94% at $4.37; GPT-5.6 Sol + Exa 45.24% at $18.97; GPT-5.6 Sol native 43.58% at $0.95; Gemini 3.5 Flash + Exa 41.36%; Grok 4.5 + Exa 38.75%; Gemini 3.5 Flash native 37.04%; Grok 4.5 native 35.54%. The no-search baseline was 2.9% legal and 7.4% finance ([Vals blog](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/web-search-index)). Vals' own text is that search, not memorization, drives its scores, which is the opposite of what LiveBrowseComp finds for BrowseComp.

Read together, the independent evidence supports a narrow conclusion: Exa leads retrieval-heavy single-query tasks (OpenBenchmarks factual, arlenkumar) and fetch-enabled agent tasks (OpenBenchmarks coding and multi-hop with fetch, AA October); Perplexity leads search-only agent tasks and BrowseComp (87 on AA); Parallel basic leads search-only multi-hop; Brave leads a snippet-relevance rubric; Keenable leads a finance-and-legal agent test with one model. Parallel's own summary of its five-engine report, "No engine led on every benchmark at every price tier", is the right reading of the field [vendor, Sep 2026] ([Parallel five-engine report](https://parallel.ai/articles/best-web-search-api-for-ai-applications)).

Vendor-run head-to-heads add volume but not agreement. ## The provider landscape is three layers deep and priced from $0.25 to $45

Twenty-plus providers sell web search to agents, but they sit in distinct layers. At the bottom are crawlers and indexes: Google, Bing, Yandex, Baidu, and the independents (Brave, Perplexity, Exa, Parallel, Keenable, Mojeek, Yep, Ceramic, Octen, DeepSeek). Official access to Google and Bing is closing: Microsoft retired all Bing Search APIs on 11 August 2025 and now sells only "Grounding with Bing Search" at $14 per 1K transactions with no access to raw content [vendor, verified] ([Microsoft lifecycle](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/lifecycle/announcements/bing-search-api-retirement); [grounding pricing](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/bing/apis/grounding-pricing)); Google's Custom Search JSON API is closed to new customers and ends on 1 January 2027 [vendor, verified] ([Google docs](https://developers.google.com/custom-search/v1/overview)). Above them sit SERP scrapers (Serper $0.30–1.00, SerpApi $1.96–25, SearchApi, Bright Data, Oxylabs, DataForSEO), which Google sued under the DMCA on 19 December 2025 [journalist, verified] ([ppc.land](https://ppc.land/google-sues-serpapi-over-search-scraping-in-copyright-lawsuit/)); then agent-facing aggregation layers (Tavily, Firecrawl, Jina, Linkup, You.com, Nimble) whose upstream sources are mostly undisclosed; and at the top the LLM platforms' built-in tools (OpenAI and Anthropic $10 per 1K, xAI $5, Gemini 3+ $14 per query) whose indexes are inferred from 2024–2025 reporting (Bing plus OAI-SearchBot for OpenAI; Brave for Anthropic, per its March 2025 subprocessor list and a `BraveSearchParams` schema) [practitioner/journalist] ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Mar/21/anthropic-use-brave/); [OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing); [Claude web search](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/web-search-tool)). Cloudflare joined as a reseller on 2 October 2026, routing to Ceramic.ai ($0.25), Exa ($7) and Linkup ($5) at list price with no markup [vendor, verified] ([Cloudflare changelog](https://developers.cloudflare.com/changelog/post/2026-10-02-introducing-web-search-api/)).

Three structural observations follow. First, list prices have clustered at $1 per 1K for fast tiers (Parallel Turbo/Fast, Perplexity Fast, Keenable at volume, Octen, Nimble), $4–5 for full-quality tiers (Exa Lite, Brave, Perplexity, Parallel Basic/Advanced, You.com, Keenable PAYG, Yep), $7–16 for Exa Auto/Deep and Tavily, and $10–14 for the first-party model tools; the sub-$1 tier (DeepSeek $0.75, Ceramic $0.25) only appeared in 2026 ([Parallel pricing](https://docs.parallel.ai/getting-started/pricing); [Keenable pricing](https://keenable.ai/pricing); [Exa pricing](https://exa.ai/pricing); [DeepSeek docs](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/guides/search)). Second, list price and all-in cost invert in agent loops: Tavily basic at $8 list was the most expensive search on AA at launch ($126 per 1K tasks) because the agent issued more searches with it, while Octen at $1 list costs $9.07 and Exa lite at $4 costs $31.20 per 1K tasks ([AA launch](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/search-api); [AA leaderboard](https://artificialanalysis.ai/agents/search-api)). Vals adds that model tokens made up about 95% of the $8.84 average task cost with Keenable, so per-query search fees are a second-order cost ([Vals](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/web_search)). Third, MCP distribution has moved to vendor-hosted Streamable-HTTP endpoints with keyless or OAuth tiers (Exa, Tavily, Firecrawl, Parallel, Keenable, You.com, Perplexity), and npm downloads rank Firecrawl (147k/week) > Exa (76k) > Brave (59k) > Perplexity (42k) > Tavily (24k) on the week to 3 November 2026, with the caveat that remote-only servers are undercounted and Brave's stdio-only server inflates its npm share [independent counts] ([npm API](https://api.npmjs.org/downloads/point/last-week/exa-mcp-server)). ## Why Keenable scores well: an owned index, 150 ms serving, and a token classifier

Keenable.ai came out of stealth on 25 August 2026 with a $26M seed led by Accel with Conviction Partners, about 15 engineers across the US and Europe, and founders Andrey Styskin (who "previously led Yandex's search, AI, and cloud division" and spent "20 years building search at Yandex and Amazon") and Matthias Petri, a German AI scientist who worked with Styskin on Alexa web search at Amazon [journalist, verified] ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/25/accel-backed-keenable-is-indexing-the-web-for-ai-agents/)). Accel's note adds that Styskin "built a 200-billion-document web index at Yandex" and led a 7,000-person organization, and that Petri built "the web grounding service behind Alexa" [investor] ([Accel](https://www.accel.com/news/our-seed-investment-in-keenable-search-infrastructure-for-better-ai-agents)); neither claim has independent corroboration. The NEEDLE post dates the first index to about February 2026, with news coverage, query syntax and snippets added over the following months [vendor, 27 Aug 2026, verified] ([NEEDLE post](https://keenable.ai/blog/needle-the-benchmark-your-search-engine-can-t-memorize)). A LlamaIndex integration released on 19 June 2026 shows the API was quietly live two months before the public launch ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/pypi/llama-index-tools-keenable/json)).

Four disclosed, checkable factors explain most of Keenable's results. The first is an owned, independent index with per-query narrowing. Styskin's only mechanistic statements are that "the cost of serving and scanning the whole internet is enormous because of the volume", that "you need to innovate on how you can narrow the search space based on your query very fast", and that index structures must be fine-tuned "for a specific task" [journalist quoting founder, verified] ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/25/accel-backed-keenable-is-indexing-the-web-for-ai-agents/)). The NEEDLE post argues that engines federating other indexes "can't meaningfully improve" beyond re-ranking and snippets, that "the major factor is index uniqueness" on rare-entity queries, that "Some systems appear to share the same index!", and that "We built Keenable as a learning machine" whose gains come "from the architecture rather than manual engineering" [vendor, verified] ([NEEDLE post](https://keenable.ai/blog/needle-the-benchmark-your-search-engine-can-t-memorize)). On its own benchmark Keenable leads only the AgenticRare slice (nDCG@5 0.494 against Perplexity 0.469 and Exa 0.460); it is second to Perplexity on Scholar (recall@10 0.712 against 0.737, Exa 0.540) and trails Exa on News (nDCG@5 0.617 against 0.648) and Finance (recall@5 0.875 against 0.910–0.917, live page) [vendor-run] ([NEEDLE board](https://keenableai.github.io/needle/)).

The second factor is latency as a product tier. The API has two modes, `realtime` ("fastest") and `pro` ("deeper retrieval"), the same index and ranking across both ([Search API reference](https://docs.keenable.ai/api-reference/search.md); [pricing](https://keenable.ai/pricing)). On NEEDLE's seven-day window ending 5 November 2026 Keenable measured **p50 154 ms, p95 317 ms with 0.0% errors**, against Exa 1.4 s / 3.2 s, Parallel 2.4 s / 3.9 s, Brave 558 / 807 ms and Tavily 1.6 / 3.2 s, all from the same GitHub Actions client issuing sequential calls [vendor-run, verified] ([NEEDLE board](https://keenableai.github.io/needle/)). The one independent latency datapoint is AA's launch article: Keenable realtime averaged **0.34 s per search call, the fastest on the board**, and finished tasks in 15.1 s against Exa auto's 26.2 s [independent, Aug 2026, verified] ([AA launch](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/search-api)). The homepage's "<250 ms p95 (US East)" is a vendor claim that the verification pass could not find in TechCrunch or Keenable's funding post. Petri's stated rationale is that lower cost and latency "lets models call search more often" [vendor on HN] ([Show HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49435555)), and Vals' observation that GPT-5.6 Sol issued a median of 99.5 legal and 58 finance searches per task with Keenable, yet cost less per task than with Exa, is consistent with that design ([Vals](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/web_search)).

The third factor, and the only fully disclosed machine-learning component, is Dynamic Snippets (rolled out 30 August 2026; described in a post dated 15 October 2026). Documents are split into overlapping chunks; a token classifier reads the query with one chunk and assigns each token a probability of being useful; decoding keeps maximal runs with score at least max(0.6·m, 0.5) where m is the document's top token score, keeps a run only if some token reaches 0.75·m, joins runs fewer than 80 characters apart, and falls back to 200 characters around the top token for about 4% of documents. Production responses fell from 135 KiB to 69 KiB (49%); in Keenable's replication of the AA setup (Stirrup, GPT-5.6 Luna, 25 turns, two tools) search-result tokens per BrowseComp question fell from 1.16M to 466k while **accuracy rose from 56.6% to 67.3%**, and the snippets "add no latency" because the classifier is small rather than an LLM summarizer [vendor, verified] ([Dynamic Snippets post](https://keenable.ai/blog/keenable-snippets-full-page-cost-reduction)). The classifier architecture is not named. This is the mechanism that most plausibly links Keenable's August AA score (67, measured before the 30 August rollout) to its October Vals result; Parallel's competitor comparison concedes the AA runs "predate Keenable's dynamic snippets change" [competitor] ([Parallel vs Keenable](https://parallel.ai/articles/keenable-vs-parallel.md)).

The fourth factor is an agent-shaped API surface: `site`, `published_after/before`, `acquired_after/before`, a point-in-time `query_time` that returns the index "as it stood at this time", up to 50 results per request at a flat price, snippet lengths from 180 to 10,000 characters, and a fetch endpoint that serves the indexed copy by default with `live=true` for unindexed URLs [vendor] ([Search reference](https://docs.keenable.ai/api-reference/search.md); [Fetch reference](https://docs.keenable.ai/api-reference/fetch.md)). The absence of language, country or safe-search filters and of a relevance score positions it as agent-only infrastructure. On Vals, where SEC EDGAR access was removed, 77.4% of finance citations in the Keenable run were sec.gov pages, against 57.6% for Exa and 53.7% for native search ([Vals](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/web_search)); the finance half of that board is largely a test of covering and ranking SEC filing pages under operator-style queries, which favours a large owned index with long result lists.

What is not disclosed is as important. Keenable has published no architecture, crawling or ranking post; whether first-stage retrieval is lexical, dense or hybrid, what the ranker trains on beyond "real search needs and agent trajectories", and what differs between `pro` and `realtime` internally are all undocumented ([keenable.ai/blog](https://keenable.ai/blog)). The 100B-document claim has no independent audit. The crawler page, first published September 2026, names two tokens, `KeenableBot` and `Keenable-User`, with RFC 9309 compliance and IP lists added in October 2026 [vendor, corrected by verification] ([bot page](https://app.keenable.ai/bot)); no recrawl cadence, politeness policy or freshness SLA is stated. Parallel notes that Keenable published no SOC 2 or other attestation as of 21 October 2026 [competitor] ([Parallel vs Keenable](https://parallel.ai/articles/keenable-vs-parallel.md)). Keenable's own NEEDLE has Perplexity ahead (74% against 72%) and has had no human-agreement audit of its GPT-6 Luna judge ([NEEDLE board](https://keenableai.github.io/needle/)). Practitioner signal is thin: one HN user reports heavy use "with no noticeable issues" after migrating from Brave, another finds it "not usable" for human-facing search with Grokipedia outranking Wikipedia, and the NEEDLE thread's reaction was that the post "devotes its entire second half to pitching Keenable itself" and asks whether it is "an independent search engine benchmark or a blog post from a search engine provider" [practitioner, Aug–Oct 2026] ([HN 49963171](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49963171); [HN 49466250](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49466250)). ## Why Exa scores well: a neural index that went hybrid, and highlights that cut queries per task

Exa was founded in 2021 as Metaphor Systems (YC S21) by Harvard friends Will Bryk and Jeff Wang, launched a consumer neural search engine in November 2022, rebranded in January 2024, and raised $17M (Lightspeed, July 2024), **$85M at a $700M valuation (Benchmark, 3 September 2025)** and **$250M at $2.2B (a16z, 20 May 2026)**; it reported passing **$100M annualized revenue with about 2 billion requests per month** on 13 October 2026 [vendor, verified; press for Series A] ([Series B](https://exa.ai/blog/announcing-series-b); [Series C](https://exa.ai/blog/announcing-series-c); [$100M post](https://exa.ai/blog/100m-run-rate); [TechCrunch 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/07/16/exa-raises-17m-lightspeed-nvidia-ycombinator-google-ai-models/)). Sacra's independent estimate had it at about $10M annualized in September 2025, growing roughly 11x year over year ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/research/exa-at-10m-growing-11x-yoy)).

The architecture timeline explains the benchmark trajectory. The original model was trained on link prediction: the text around a link is shown with the link hidden and the model predicts the document it points to, which Bryk calls "neural PageRank"; in January 2025 he described Exa as "neural all the way" with rerankers over 10–1,000 candidates and LLMs as relevance labelers because human labelers "tended to match keywords rather than meaning" [vendor] ([Latent Space](https://www.latent.space/p/exa); [TechCrunch 2024](https://techcrunch.com/2024/07/16/exa-raises-17m-lightspeed-nvidia-ycombinator-google-ai-models/)). The custom vector database (December 2024) truncates 4,096-float Matryoshka embeddings to 256 dimensions, binary-quantizes to one bit per dimension, partitions documents into 100,000 clusters, keeps inverted indexes for filterable fields, and reranks extra candidates with uncompressed vectors [vendor, verified] ([vector DB post](https://exa.ai/blog/building-web-scale-vector-db)). The Exacluster (144 H200s, about $5M) was added for embedding and pretraining in 2025 ([Exacluster](https://exa.ai/blog/meet-the-exacluster)). Exa 2.0 (10 October 2025) introduced a new embedding model trained for a month on that cluster, Fast (<350 ms p50) and Deep modes ([Exa 2.0](https://exa.ai/blog/exa-api-2-0)); the exa-d framework (January 2026) stores "hundreds of billions of pages" in Lance format on S3 with embeddings regenerated on change ([exa-d](https://exa.ai/blog/exa-d)); Instant (February 2026) claimed sub-200 ms ([Exa Instant](https://exa.ai/blog/exa-instant)); and the April 2026 "Composing a Search Engine" post shows the retrieval DAG now fuses a dense ANN worker group with a sparse retrieval node over an inverted index via reciprocal rank fusion with k = 60, then applies a freshness model, domain diversity, and relevance and authority rerankers [vendor, verified; the post says "sparse/inverted", not "BM25"] ([Composing post](https://exa.ai/blog/composing-a-search-engine)). Exa 3.0 (19 October 2026) added a larger embedding model, made Dynamic Highlights generally available ("95% fewer tokens than full page text"), added Lite at $4 per 1K as the only genuinely new search type (Auto dates from December 2024 and Deep from November 2025), and expanded the index with 1.27B e-commerce listings and 211M places [vendor, verified] ([Exa 3.0](https://exa.ai/blog/exa-3); [changelog](https://docs.exa.ai/changelog)). Index-size statements moved from "billions of documents" (March 2025) to "tens of billions" (October 2025), "500 billion URLs tracked" (May 2026), "80B pages / 1.4T URLs" (August 2026, secondary and unverified) and "2 trillion URLs tracked, 100 billion pages served" (October 2026); the units differ and the figures should be treated as marketing-grade.

Exa's own explanation of its scores is that long natural-language queries are embedded directly, that the index stores parsed page text plus embeddings so results carry usable content rather than SERP snippets, that rerankers trade compute for quality, and that better retrieval means fewer calls: "20% to 50% fewer search queries per task than other providers", with AA's 7.60 queries per task for Exa auto against 9.51–15.4 for rivals [vendor relaying independent data] ([Exa on AA](https://exa.ai/blog/artificial-analysis-exa-3-best-search-api)). The drift from "neural all the way" to an explicitly hybrid design with a sparse node and an authority reranker is the most plausible explanation for improved exact-match performance, and the coincidence of hybrid retrieval (April 2026) and Dynamic Highlights (August–October 2026) with the AA rise from 74 to 80 suggests content packaging mattered as much as retrieval. Independent and competitor evidence that supports the vendor story: AA ranks Exa auto first at 80 with the highest DSQA F1 (86); OpenBenchmarks has Exa fast at 99.3% on factual lookup and Exa deep first on coding-agent retrieval with fetch (83.0) and multi-hop with fetch (48.2); arlenkumar has Exa hit@5 at 80.9% against SerpAPI 69.2% (p ≈ 0.001); Vals has Claude Fable 5 with Exa at 48.45%, within 0.63 of the top at less than half the cost, with a significant +6.51-point finance effect over native search ([AA leaderboard](https://artificialanalysis.ai/agents/search-api); [OpenBenchmarks](https://openbenchmarks.com/web-search); [arlenkumar](https://arlenkumar.com/bench/leaderboards/web-search); [Vals](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/web_search)). Even Parallel credits Exa with "semantic discovery where vector ranking is the priority" and with leading on WideSearch at the low-cost tier ([Parallel vs Exa](https://parallel.ai/compare/exa-vs-parallel)).

The known weaknesses are equally well attested. On keyword-exact and code-specific queries, a practitioner scorecard notes that "Neural/semantic search can behave unpredictably on highly specific technical queries — traditional keyword search sometimes returns more reliable results for code-related lookups" and that highlights are "a hint, not ground truth" [practitioner, Mar–Jun 2026] ([Rhumb via dev.to](https://dev.to/supertrained/exa-vs-tavily-vs-serper-vs-brave-search-for-ai-agents-an-score-comparison-2l1g)); a tiny community benchmark scored Exa fastest (137 ms) but lowest on keyword relevance (3.2/10, with "Monarch: an API to your supercomputer" as the top hit for "latest AI frameworks 2025") [practitioner, May 2026] ([pi-search-multi](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/pi-search-multi@1.0.1/benchmark/benchmark-report.md)); and Exa's own comparison page concedes "Brave is the strongest general web index we tested, while Exa leads on entity retrieval and groundedness" [vendor] ([Exa vs Brave](https://exa.ai/versus/brave)). Cost per task is the second weakness: in Parallel's frontier run Exa Auto cost $971 per 1K BrowseComp questions against Perplexity's $275, and on AA Exa auto's search cost ($53.17) is six times Octen's ($9.07) for three index points ([Parallel benchmarks](https://parallel.ai/benchmarks); [AA leaderboard](https://artificialanalysis.ai/agents/search-api)). Measured latency exceeds advertised: Instant's "sub-200 ms" measured 335–361 ms p50 from Parallel's client and 398 ms mean on OpenBenchmarks; NEEDLE's current window has Exa at p50 1.4 s / p95 3.2 s ([Parallel vs Exa](https://parallel.ai/compare/exa-vs-parallel); [NEEDLE](https://keenableai.github.io/needle/)). Perplexity's agent-versus-agent harness places Exa Agent last on all four suites ([search_evals](https://github.com/perplexityai/search_evals)). Exa's status page shows six Search or Agents API incidents between 16 September and 3 November 2026, including a 2h21m search-latency incident and a 4h56m Agents API failure, with September Search API uptime at 99.97% [vendor] ([status.exa.ai](https://status.exa.ai/history/1)). Practitioner complaints are commercial: per-result charges beyond the top 10, Websets as a separate $49–449 per month product, zero data retention as a paid enterprise feature, hard 429s at quota, and a Cloudflare 403 from datacenter IPs on both the API and the hosted MCP endpoint [practitioner] ([Reddit r/AI_Agents snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1tn44cu/snapshots/2026-05-29T19%3A16%3A10.134322Z); [HN 49963171](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49963171); [exa-mcp-server issue 446](https://github.com/exa-labs/exa-mcp-server/issues/446)). Exa's own evals post also admits that LLM-judge grading carries "higher cost, more grading variance, dependence on the grading prompt" and a "verification gap" ([Evals at Exa](https://exa.ai/blog/evals-at-exa)).

The final observation on Exa is that its published numbers are strongest where Exa also wrote the benchmark (People and Company Search, WebCode, publications, ATLAS), while on third-party-designed BrowseComp its standing runs from 70% (Parallel's 50-question Sol harness) to 83 (AA's 200-question Luna harness) to 91.8% (Exa 3.0's own post, harness unstated). The spread is mostly harness, model and tier, not retrieval.

## Common Crawl is 2% of the commercial claims and has none of the serving layers

Common Crawl's October 2026 main crawl (CC-MAIN-2026-43) fetched **2.16 billion pages (371.19 TiB uncompressed) from 41.4 million hosts and 34.3 million registered domains between 5 and 18 October**, of which 830.1 million URLs (about 38%) had not appeared in any prior crawl [measured, verified] ([CC October 2026](https://commoncrawl.org/blog/october-2026-crawl-archive-now-available)). That is down about 29% from the 3.0B pages of CC-MAIN-2025-05 ([CC January 2025](https://commoncrawl.org/blog/january-2025-crawl-archive-now-available)), and the per-crawl counts for 2026 run 1.97–2.3B against yearly averages of 2.69B (2024) and 2.53B (2025); Bright Data's analysis of CC's own numbers declines to attribute the decline to publisher blocking alone ([Bright Data](https://brightdata.com/blog/web-data/internet-archive-vs-common-crawl-vs-web-archive)). Selection ranks domains by harmonic centrality (chosen over PageRank because "it is deemed better at avoiding spam"), budgets URLs per domain in proportion to that score, caps each crawl at a fixed URL maximum, and revisits about 50% of URLs from prior crawls; CC's director describes the result as "an academic sampling of the web" and the Mozilla report concludes the data "consists of samples of samples" [independent, 2024, verified] ([Mozilla Foundation report](https://assets.mofoprod.net/network/documents/Common_Crawl_Mozilla_Foundation_2024.pdf)). CCBot does not execute JavaScript, honours robots.txt and `Crawl-delay`, waits "few seconds" between requests to a site, and therefore "Highly ranked domains tend to be underrepresented" by design ([CC FAQ](https://commoncrawl.org/faq); [CC domain statistics](https://commoncrawl.github.io/cc-crawl-statistics/plots/domains)). English is 42.56% of the October 2026 crawl ([CC language statistics](https://commoncrawl.github.io/cc-crawl-statistics/plots/languages)).

Quantified against the commercial claims, one CC crawl is about 7% of Brave's 30B, 5% of Brave's or Ceramic's 40B, 24% of Mojeek's 9B, roughly 2% of Exa's or Keenable's 100B, 1% of Perplexity's 200B URLs, and well under 1% of Google's "hundreds of billions of webpages ... well over 100,000,000 gigabytes" ([Google How Search Works](https://www.google.com/search/howsearchworks/how-search-works/organizing-information/)). The cumulative archive, described as "over 300 billion web pages" by AWS and about 9.5 PB by mid-2023, is in Google's order of magnitude only if duplicates and revisits are ignored ([AWS Open Data](https://registry.opendata.aws/commoncrawl/)). Cadence is the sharper gap: CC's revisit interval is monthly at best and most URLs are never revisited, while Brave claims more than 100 million pages added or refreshed per day (a 2.16B-page crawl fetched over 14 days is about 150 million fetches per day while crawling, or about 70 million per day averaged over the monthly cadence, by arithmetic), Perplexity claims "tens of thousands of indexing operations per second" with ML-scheduled recrawl, Octen claims indexing "within 5 minutes", and the Open Web Index project crawled daily with daily index deltas precisely because CC's dumps are monthly [vendor claims; OWI academic] ([Brave growth post](https://brave.com/blog/search-api-growth/); [Perplexity blog](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/blog/architecting-and-evaluating-an-ai-first-search-api); [Hendriksen et al. 2024](https://elib.dlr.de/210780/1/hendriksen_2024.pdf)).

What CC does not provide is everything that makes a search API usable at query time. Its two indexes are URL and metadata lookups (the CDXJ server and the Parquet "URL Index" with url, languages, crawl, subset and WARC offset columns); there is no full-text retrieval, and the public CDX API is "frequently abused and therefore heavily rate limited" ([CC URL Index](https://commoncrawl.org/blog/the-columnar-index-is-now-the-url-index); [CC FAQ](https://commoncrawl.org/faq)). There are no ranking signals beyond host-level harmonic centrality and PageRank, where Perplexity describes lexical plus semantic candidate retrieval, prefilters, embedding scorers and cross-encoder rerankers at document and sub-document level, and Brave cites behavioural signals from "130+ million real humans" via its opt-in Web Discovery Project ([CC webgraph](https://commoncrawl.github.io/cc-webgraph-statistics/); [Brave API page](https://brave.com/search/api/)). There is no spam, quality or boilerplate filtering: FineWeb found WET plaintext "retained too much boilerplate and menu text", and its pipeline shrank 96 snapshots from ~36T tokens after base filtering to ~15T [academic, 2024] ([FineWeb](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.17557v2)). There is no snippet or passage extraction, no JavaScript rendering (Google renders "just as a browser does"; ClueWeb22 includes browser renderings), and no low-latency serving: the only production-grade CC search, Elastic ChatNoir, indexed 1.6B CC pages (2015) on 124 Elasticsearch data nodes with a 9.7 TB index plus 28.8 TB document store (115.6 TB at 3x replication), answering in about 600 ms warm and 2,100 ms cold [academic, 2018, verified] ([Bevendorff et al. 2018](https://downloads.webis.de/publications/papers/bevendorff_2018.pdf)). A 2018 hobby estimate put a BM25 index of a 3.2B-page snapshot at about 13.2 TB, under $50 of EC2 compute to build but about $400 per month of S3 storage, with the caveat that S3 random-seek latency makes serving costly ([fulmicoton](https://fulmicoton.com/posts/commoncrawl/)). Scaling ChatNoir's ratios to the 2026 crawl implies roughly 13 TB of index and 39 TB of document store before replication; that is arithmetic, not a measurement.

The projects that tried to build search on CC converged on the same lesson. Stract used CC "in the early stages of development" before writing its own crawler and was archived in April 2026; Alexandria.org is CC-based and unreachable; the EU's €8.5M OpenWebSearch.eu project stated that "it is often cheaper to crawl and index only a smaller collection focused on a downstream application scenario than to build and maintain an index for a general collection the size of the Common Crawl" and ended in February 2026 without a search API [academic/vendor] ([Stract README](https://github.com/StractOrg/stract/blob/main/README.md); [Hendriksen et al.](https://elib.dlr.de/210780/1/hendriksen_2024.pdf); [OWS.EU wrap-up](https://openwebsearch.eu/title-42-months-in-the-making-openwebsearch-eu-project-end-results-look-promising/)). CC's dominant downstream success is LLM pretraining (C4, RefinedWeb, FineWeb, DCLM-Pool's 240T tokens), not retrieval. No source, vendor or third-party, states that Exa, Keenable, Brave, Perplexity or Mojeek uses CC for seeding or training; all describe their own crawlers, and absence of mention is not denial.

Blocking adds a structural hole. Known Agents reports that **26% of the top 1,000 websites had robots.txt rules for CCBot as of 4 November 2026**, classifying it as an "AI Data Scraper" [independent tracker, verified] ([Known Agents](https://knownagents.com/agents/ccbot)); a Cloudflare Radar-derived parse ranks CCBot second only to Bytespider by block-to-allow ratio (2.61:1) [vendor blog, Sep 2026] ([TechnologyChecker](https://technologychecker.io/blog/robots-txt-ai-crawlers-blocking-report)); a claim that 37.4% of 107 prominent publishers block it could not be verified. Because CCBot is categorized as a training scraper, its block rate is set by the AI-training debate, and the blocked content (news, tech press, large platforms) is exactly what a search API needs most. Cloudflare's default blocking of AI crawlers from 1 July 2025, its August 2025 de-listing of Perplexity as a verified bot over an alleged undeclared crawler at 3–6M requests per day (Perplexity attributed the traffic to BrowserBase at under 45,000 requests per day), and its September 2026 default blocking of AI agents and "mixed-use" crawlers on ad-supported pages with "Pay Per Use" for publishers raise the cost of independent crawling for everyone [vendor/journalist, verified] ([Cloudflare July 2025](https://blog.cloudflare.com/content-independence-day-no-ai-crawl-without-compensation/); [Cloudflare on Perplexity](https://blog.cloudflare.com/perplexity-is-using-stealth-undeclared-crawlers-to-evade-website-no-crawl-directives/); [Perplexity reply](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/blog/agents-or-bots-making-sense-of-ai-on-the-open-web); [TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/cloudflare-block-ai-crawlers-pay-publishers)). Styskin's summary of what it costs to do what CC does not, "Don't ask — it is painfully expensive", is the commercial reality the open corpus leaves unsolved ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/25/accel-backed-keenable-is-indexing-the-web-for-ai-agents/)).

## Methodology caveats: one product spans 33.7% to 91.8% on one benchmark

The same product's BrowseComp score spans 33.7% to 91.8% (Exa) or 32% to 75% (Parallel Turbo) depending on who ran it, and each gap maps to an identifiable change in agent model, reasoning effort, sample, tool budget, judge, tier or scoring rule, not to a change in the index. Parallel Turbo scored 51% in July 2026 (GPT-5.4 agent and judge, 1,266 questions, best observed across sessions), 32% in September (GPT-5.6 Luna low, 50 questions, unnamed judge) and 75 on AA's BrowseComp component (GPT-5.6 Luna medium, 200 hard questions, 25 turns) ([Parallel vs SerpApi](https://parallel.ai/compare/serpapi-vs-parallel); [Parallel benchmarks](https://parallel.ai/benchmarks); [AA launch](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/search-api)). Exa runs from 33.7% (Instant, Parallel July) through 36% and 70% (Auto, Parallel September low-cost and frontier), 0.127 and 0.336 (Perplexity's repo with claude-opus-4-5-thinking and gpt-5-medium), 74 and 83 (AA August and October) to 91.8% (Exa 3.0's own post) ([search_evals commit](https://github.com/perplexityai/search_evals/commit/39d576b); [Exa 3.0](https://exa.ai/blog/exa-3)). Perplexity runs from .371 (its own 2025 launch eval) through 46% and 74% (Parallel) to 86–87 (AA) and 91.5 (Exa's run, second-hand) ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/10/20/exa-3-0-search-api-lite/)). Parallel's own BrowseComp article says its three tables "measure different things and should not be compared directly" ([Parallel BrowseComp explainer](https://parallel.ai/articles/browsecomp-benchmark)). Agent reasoning effort is the single biggest lever: Parallel's Luna-low tier scores about 30 points below its Sol-high tier on identical search APIs.

Five methodological problems recur. The first is self-grading and circularity. Google released Gemini Deep Research and DeepSearchQA together and topped its own leaderboard; implicator.ai called it "a ruler that measures 11 inches" [journalist] ([implicator.ai](https://www.implicator.ai/google-turns-research-agents-into-infrastructure-and-writes-the-test-theyre-graded-on)). The SimpleQA Verified authors note that several are at Google and that Gemini 2.5 Pro both tops their table and was used in filtering. Every vendor table ranks the vendor first. Even AA uses the candidate model as grader, which its methodology page states as a caveat ([AA methodology](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/search-api)). Keenable's NEEDLE "ultimate" ceiling is pooled from the engines in the comparison, so it cannot reward a relevant page no engine returned, and its GPT-6 Luna judge has had no human-agreement audit ([NEEDLE board](https://keenableai.github.io/needle/)).

The second is unpublished or inconsistent harnesses. Parallel acknowledges that its judge model and prompt, tool-call budget, sampled question IDs, system prompt, runs per configuration, run-to-run variance and contamination filtering are all unpublished, and that "we make the Parallel Search API and grade our own product" ([Parallel how-we-evaluate](https://parallel.ai/articles/how-we-evaluate-web-search-apis)). Linkup's 2025 table used competitors' self-published numbers rather than re-running them; SerpApi's table copies Tavily's and Parallel's figures; Brave's competitor rows mix F1 and Acc* from other vendors' runs; Perplexity represented all "SERP based" providers by Tavily alone ([Linkup](https://www.linkup.so/blog/linkup-establishes-sota-performance-on-simpleqa); [SerpApi post](https://dev.to/adarshdigievo/serpapi-achieves-976-accuracy-on-simpleqa-verified-1mi5); [Brave](https://brave.com/blog/ai-grounding/)). Firecrawl reported "the better of two sessions"; Parallel's July run reported "best observed across sessions"; LiveResearchBench configured each system "to maximize performance" ([Firecrawl glossary](https://www.firecrawl.dev/glossary/web-search-apis/simpleqa-benchmark); [LiveResearchBench](https://huggingface.co/papers/2510.14240)). You.com's harness README states two different synthesis models for the same results, and Exa's ATLAS post names GPT-5.6 Luna in one place and GPT-6 Luna in another ([web-search-api-evals](https://github.com/youdotcom-oss/web-search-api-evals); [Exa ATLAS](https://exa.ai/blog/atlas-benchmark)). Vendors systematically pick the competitor tier that loses: Exa `lite` against Tavily `advanced`, Parallel Turbo against Exa Instant, Tavily's repo calling Brave's snippet endpoint rather than LLM Context.

The third is sample size and missing uncertainty. Parallel's 50 BrowseComp questions imply roughly ±13 points at 95% (1.96 x 6.5); OpenBenchmarks' multi-hop board has 45 questions; AA publishes no confidence intervals or run counts; AIMultiple's top four overlap; Vals gives standard errors per category but none on headline scores and none at all for Keenable ([Parallel BrowseComp explainer](https://parallel.ai/articles/browsecomp-benchmark); [TinyFish](https://www.tinyfish.ai/blog/search-api-benchmark); [Vals](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/web_search)).

The fourth is judge error and contamination. Brave's human review found the LLM judge wrong in 167 of 292 answers it had marked incorrect ([Brave AI Grounding](https://brave.com/blog/ai-grounding/)); TREC RAG 2025's automatic relevance judgments reached only 0.30–0.34 agreement with NIST assessors ([TREC 2025 RAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09891v1)); Search Arena's expert users agreed with crowd votes 68% of the time, and the crowd rewarded response length (β 0.334), citation count (β 0.209) and irrelevant citations almost as much as supporting ones (0.27 against 0.29) ([Search Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05334v2)). BrowseComp "questions still leak" (verbatim pages were found); Brave excluded huggingface.co/datasets URLs and pages containing "simpleqa" and says other providers do not consistently filter; SerpApi dropped 40 of 1,000 SimpleQA Verified questions "where search results exposed benchmark answers"; FinSearchComp's reference answers are published online; Keenable found 12 contaminated WideSearch samples ([Parallel explainer](https://parallel.ai/articles/browsecomp-benchmark); [Brave](https://brave.com/blog/ai-grounding/); [SerpApi](https://dev.to/adarshdigievo/serpapi-achieves-976-accuracy-on-simpleqa-verified-1mi5); [Parallel FinSearchComp](https://parallel.ai/articles/finsearchcomp-benchmark.md); [Keenable SELECT](https://keenable.ai/blog/select-the-tail-from-the-web)). Unfiltered SimpleQA runs, which most vendor runs are, carry an unknown contamination bonus.

The fifth is product-version drift and live-API irreproducibility. Exa 2.x to 3.0, Brave Search to LLM Context, Tavily basic to advanced and ultra-fast, Perplexity Search to Fast Search, and Keenable before and after Dynamic Snippets all changed scores without any re-scoring of old runs; AA and OpenBenchmarks date their boards, most vendor pages do not. OpenBenchmarks' own finding that later-benchmarked vendors score higher for reasons unrelated to quality means its factual-lookup ranking should not be used until a re-run ([OpenBenchmarks](https://openbenchmarks.com/web-search)). AA's time-per-task is a modelled quantity (output tokens divided by canonical output speed plus measured search time), not a measurement, and no evaluator publishes p95 for agent-task duration; the only cross-vendor percentile tables are NEEDLE's and Perplexity's 2025 post, both vendor-run ([AA methodology](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/search-api)). The one independent partial replication that exists is encouraging on reproducibility: fastCRW rebuilt AA's loop from published constants and landed its Firecrawl control at 74.5% against AA's 73 on the Omniscience subset ([fastCRW](https://fastcrw.com/blog/aa-search-index-omniscience-benchmark)).

The practical rule that falls out of this is that only results satisfying "same harness, same judge, same date, published settings" are comparable, which today means AA and OpenBenchmarks, with Vals comparable within its own board; every vendor table is an upper bound for that vendor under its best configuration; and any BrowseComp comparison across sources that does not match the agent model and effort is meaningless.

## What the external evidence says about a reranking proxy over Tavily

This section reads the external evidence against the three evaluations in the repository's README: an Elo leaderboard on 200 agent and chat requests judged by Claude Haiku 5.5 and gpt-6-luna, SimpleQA top-3 on 1,200 questions, and DeepSearchQA with a 5-step agent on 500 tasks. The README figures referred to below are `harder` at 1083/1109 Elo, 96.5% on SimpleQA top-3 and DSQA F1 66.8 (described there as "level with Keenable") against Tavily advanced's 954/944, 96.3% and 60.4, at about $0.09 and 7–11 s per request against $0.016 and 3–5 s ([README](../README.md)); the benchmark reports it cites are not in this checkout.

**SimpleQA's Wikipedia composition and the Wikipedia-pin finding.** Wikipedia is a source for about 81% of SimpleQA questions (3.5k of 4.3k) [academic] ([SimpleQA paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04368v1)), which is why the README's Wikipedia-pin result (keeping a Wikipedia hit found in the first three pool positions in place helps SimpleQA by about a point and nothing else, and a prompt hint to prefer reference sources did not help) is exactly what the composition predicts: any provider whose top-k reliably includes the relevant Wikipedia page scores at or above 90%, and the README's 96.3–96.5% sits inside the saturated 91–97% band Parallel found for all ten of its configurations and the 87.7–99.3% band on OpenBenchmarks' factual board ([Parallel SimpleQA explainer](https://parallel.ai/articles/simpleqa-benchmark.md); [OpenBenchmarks](https://openbenchmarks.com/web-search)). Pinning Wikipedia hard-codes the benchmark's source bias, and Search Arena's finding that real users disfavour Wikipedia citations (β −0.071) while rewarding community, tech and social platforms is the strongest external reason to keep it off for agent traffic ([Search Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05334v2)). Google's statement that tool-enabled SimpleQA Verified is "near perfect" means the metric cannot discriminate between `better`, `harder` and Tavily advanced; a 0.2-point gap is two or three questions on 1,200 and within judge noise ([SimpleQA Verified](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07968v1)). Keiro measured answer presence on SimpleQA at 92.0% with full-page content, 75.6% when trimmed to about 900 BM25-selected tokens and 59.4% from titles and snippets alone, concluding that token budget rather than ranking dominates [vendor] ([Keiro lite-search-benchmark](https://github.com/Keirolabs-API/lite-search-benchmark)); the README's finding that a 600-character rerank window dropped the answer-bearing page in 5 of 20 failures, fixed by a 4,000-character window, is the same effect. Practitioners report that Tavily's results "rely on multiple search backends, so result ordering can shift" with "no way to force a specific search engine or verify which backend was used" [practitioner] ([Rhumb via dev.to](https://dev.to/supertrained/exa-vs-tavily-vs-serper-vs-brave-search-for-ai-agents-an-score-comparison-2l1g)), and Perplexity labelled it "SERP-based" ([Perplexity blog](https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/blog/architecting-and-evaluating-an-ai-first-search-api)); the README's observation that `include_published_date` and `exclude_domains` change what Tavily retrieves (6.2–6.5 of 10 top URLs shared; 43% of non-social pages retained) is independent evidence of the same fan-out, since a request parameter that changes the candidate set is the signature of aggregation rather than of a single ranked index.

**Retrieval-only versus agent-loop harnesses.** The external pattern is that reranking and pool-widening pay off most where lists are judged and least where a model runs a long loop: OpenBenchmarks' winners change with tool setup, AA's agents compensate for weaker providers by searching more (Firecrawl 15.4 queries per task against Exa's 7.6), DeepWeb-Bench attributes only 12–14% of frontier-model errors to retrieval, and LiveBrowseComp finds retrieved evidence used less than a third of the time ([OpenBenchmarks](https://openbenchmarks.com/web-search); [Exa on AA](https://exa.ai/blog/artificial-analysis-exa-3-best-search-api); [DeepWeb-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21482); [LiveBrowseComp](https://huggingface.co/papers/2605.28721)). The README's evaluations split the same way (the Elo leaderboard and SimpleQA top-3 judge returned lists; only the DeepSearchQA run is an agent loop, and a tight one at 5 steps against AA's 25 turns and 500 tasks against AA's 900), and its gains follow the pattern: about 130 Elo but 6.4 F1 points, after an earlier `harder` that the README says "stayed at Tavily's level on DeepSearchQA" until the social-media-hybrid change. The upper bound on what retrieval quality can do inside an agent loop is BrowseComp-Plus's 14-point jump for GPT-5 from BM25 to a dense retriever over the same corpus, which also cut search calls by one to three per query ([BrowseComp-Plus](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06600v1)); a 6.4-point DSQA gain is a plausible fraction of that ceiling for a proxy that cannot change the index. A 5-step budget is also unusually short for DeepSearchQA, whose exhaustive-set answers produced a 15-point gap between F1 and fully-correct rates even for Gemini's own research agent ([DeepSearchQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20975v1)); the proxy's wider pool is likely worth more under a short budget than under AA's 25 turns, where the agent can recover coverage by issuing more queries itself.

**What a reranking proxy over an aggregator can gain.** The evidence supports four gains, all of which the README reports. Per-call precision from listwise reranking shows up wherever lists are judged, and the list-judging boards (AIMultiple and arlenkumar, independent; NEEDLE, Keenable-run) are where Tavily ranks lowest (NEEDLE 43%, arlenkumar hit@5 49.4%), so there is headroom ([NEEDLE](https://keenableai.github.io/needle/); [arlenkumar](https://arlenkumar.com/bench/leaderboards/web-search)). Coverage within the upstream's reach can be widened by rephrasing and targeted calls, which is a cheap version of what Exa Deep does ("optimized query expansion" over Instant) and what Parallel Advanced does ("querying, reranking, and compressing results") ([Exa Deep](https://exa.ai/blog/exa-deep); [Parallel Search API post](https://parallel.ai/blog/parallel-search-api)). Date-awareness in the reranker and a `time_range`-driven fresh call address the freshness gap that FreshQA, LiveBrowseComp and NEEDLE's News slice measure and that every own-index vendor now exposes as a product feature (Perplexity's freshness model, Exa's `maxAgeHours`, Keenable's `acquired_at`, Brave's 24-hour filter). Demoting social-media pages while keeping them in the pool is consistent with Search Arena's finding that community and social sources carry positive preference coefficients: the README's `harder-clean`, which excluded those domains outright, lost about 35 Elo, and the hybrid recovered it.

**What it cannot gain.** The proxy inherits Tavily's index coverage and freshness, which the independent boards place mid-to-low on hard retrieval (AA 66 at launch, tenth of eleven; OpenBenchmarks coding-agent 60.0 against Exa deep's 83.0; multi-hop 41.1), and Keenable's argument that "you cannot fix retrieval you do not own" applies: no rerank can surface a page the upstream never returns ([AA launch](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/search-api); [OpenBenchmarks](https://openbenchmarks.com/web-search); [NEEDLE post](https://keenable.ai/blog/needle-the-benchmark-your-search-engine-can-t-memorize)). It adds latency rather than removing it: 7–11 s per request against NEEDLE's p50 of 154 ms for Keenable, 464 ms for Exa lite and 1.6 s for Tavily itself, and AA's data show per-call latency compounding over 7–15 searches per task ([NEEDLE](https://keenableai.github.io/needle/)). It multiplies upstream cost by roughly five to six (about $0.09 against $0.016 per request), and in an AA-style loop where the agent issued about 16 Tavily searches per task (Tavily basic's $126 per 1K tasks at $8 per 1K credits), the proxy's per-request cost would put search spend an order of magnitude above Exa auto's $53 per 1K tasks; this is arithmetic on the README's and AA's numbers, not a measurement. It does not change payload shape: the levers that moved the AA board in 2026 were token-efficient result formats, with Exa's Dynamic Highlights (74 to 80), Brave's LLM Context (65 to 75) and Keenable's Dynamic Snippets (56.6% to 67.3% BrowseComp in its own replication), and Vals' finding that model tokens are about 95% of task cost says this is where the economics are; a proxy that returns Tavily's payload unchanged leaves that lever untouched, although it could in principle add passage selection of its own ([AA leaderboard](https://artificialanalysis.ai/agents/search-api); [Dynamic Snippets post](https://keenable.ai/blog/keenable-snippets-full-page-cost-reduction); [Vals](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/web_search)).

**Which published numbers are comparable to the README's.** Strictly, none. The "level with Keenable" statement is internal to the README's own DeepSearchQA harness (500 tasks, 5-step agent, its own judge); the externally published Keenable DSQA number is AA's F1 of 70 for both modes in August 2026 under GPT-5.6 Luna with 25 turns on 900 tasks, before Dynamic Snippets, and Keenable has published no DSQA figure of its own ([AA launch](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/search-api)). The README's DSQA F1 of 66.8 for `harder` and 60.4 for Tavily advanced cannot be placed on AA's 74–86 scale because the step budget, model and judge differ; what can be compared is the shape of the gap, and the README's 6.4-point lift over its own Tavily baseline is about half the 12-point spread AA observes between its weakest and strongest commercial provider. The README's SimpleQA top-3 is an answer-presence metric with a 4,000-character window; the nearest published analogues are Keiro's answer-presence figures (92.0% full crawl) and OpenBenchmarks' AR@1/AR@5 columns, not the end-to-end extraction accuracies that Tavily (93.3% with gpt-4.1), You.com's harness (Tavily 88.53%) or Parallel (Tavily 92–94% on Verified) report, which are lower because they require the reader to extract the right answer rather than merely have it in context ([tavily-search-evals](https://github.com/tavily-ai/tavily-search-evals); [web-search-api-evals](https://github.com/youdotcom-oss/web-search-api-evals)). The README's Elo leaderboard belongs to the same family as AIMultiple's LLM-judged top-5 relevance and NEEDLE's snippet-only nDCG@5 (which uses the same gpt-6-luna judge model as the README's second judge), but each uses its own queries and rubric; the one lesson that transfers is Search Arena's, that LLM and human judges of lists reward length, citation count and source type in ways that do not track task success ([Search Arena](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05334v2)). If the author wants an externally comparable number, the route is AA's published constants (GPT-5.6 Luna medium, 10 results, search plus fetch, 25 turns, DSQA full split), which fastCRW showed can be reproduced to within 1.5 points on one component ([fastCRW](https://fastcrw.com/blog/aa-search-index-omniscience-benchmark)), or OpenBenchmarks' open harness code; both would rank the proxy against the same providers it is implicitly compared with.

## Open gaps the research could not close

Several questions remain open because sources were unreachable or do not exist. Reddit was blocked from the research session by every route tried (old.reddit.com refused, reddit.com inaccessible to the search user agent, DuckDuckGo and Bing fallbacks returned bot challenges or irrelevant results, raw curl returned 403), so only one Reddit thread, an r/AI_Agents post about Exa pricing via an archived snapshot, informs the practitioner sections; X returned HTTP 402 and xcancel HTTP 451, so no X posts were collected, and LinkedIn was not reachable. Keenable's live NEEDLE host (needle.keenable.ai) did not resolve from the wave-2 session, although the GitHub Pages board did; Brave's status page did not resolve; Yep returned 403; Keiro's agenticsearch.ai returned 503; Sacra returned 503 during verification, so its "does not maintain its own search index" characterization of Tavily could not be re-read; Anthropic's subprocessor page rendered only a title, so Brave's current status as Anthropic's search provider rests on March 2025 reporting; Arena.ai's live Search Arena leaderboard returned 403, so no 2026 standings were obtained and the April 2025 figures (1142 and 1136) are unverifiable against the paper's 1150.6 and 1150.1.

On the leaderboards, AA's October 2026 scores for Tavily, Linkup, Keenable, TinyFish, Parallel turbo/fast/basic, Perplexity low, Brave web, Nimble lite and You.com snippets are behind a client-side filter and unknown; AA's update cadence, run dates per provider, the reason its model-cost column fell three to four times between August and October, and whether Stirrup has a public repository are all unstated. Vals names Parallel and Tavily as partners but shows no scores for them, publishes no significance test for Keenable, and does not say whether partners pay. OpenBenchmarks' operators and funding are undisclosed and its factual board awaits a re-run. No independent study runs the same providers through both a retrieval-only and an agent-loop harness and reports rank correlation; no evaluator publishes p95 for agent-task duration; no source measured the SimpleQA score delta from excluding Wikipedia or the fraction answerable from the top Wikipedia result alone.

On the providers, Keenable has disclosed no architecture, ranking, recrawl cadence or SOC 2 attestation, and its 100B-document claim, like Exa's 100B pages and 2T URLs, Perplexity's 200B URLs, Brave's 40B and Yep's 100B, has no independent audit. Whether Exa, Keenable, Brave or Perplexity used Common Crawl to seed URL discovery is undisclosed; whether Tavily's post-acquisition stack includes an owned index is unknown; Tavily's SealQA and SimpleQA figures from its August 2026 post, Firecrawl's competitor scores, Linkup's harness details and Exa's per-provider ATLAS and 3.0 competitor numbers exist only as images or not at all. The Brave 38.3% BrowseComp figure from Parallel's July run appears on one Parallel page and could not be confirmed on the others. The Parallel November 2025 launch benchmark is offline. The status of Google v. SerpApi after the February 2026 motion to dismiss, Cloudflare's own text for its September 2026 defaults, and any publisher-licensing economics (Linkup, Parallel's Index, Perplexity's Comet Plus payouts) were not found. Finally, the README's own benchmark reports were not available in this checkout, so the comparability analysis above rests on the README's descriptions of its harnesses rather than on the harness code or result files.

## Conclusion

For a reranking proxy the next lever the external evidence points to is not more reranking but passage selection, since token efficiency is where Vals says about 95% of task cost sits and where the 2026 movers on the Artificial Analysis board (Exa's Dynamic Highlights, Brave's LLM Context, Keenable's Dynamic Snippets) earned their points ([Vals](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/web_search); [AA leaderboard](https://artificialanalysis.ai/agents/search-api)). The honest comparison for any new entrant is a dated run under Artificial Analysis's or OpenBenchmarks' published constants, because every other number in this market, including the ones in this report, is a snapshot of one harness on one day.
